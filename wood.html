6.1

Wood as a building material

A tree's wood suitability depends on: the species, its growth characteristics, and how the trunk is cut and processed. Some woods are prized for furniture and millwork; others are mostly for framing.

CategoryWhat it isExamples
LumberSawn and planed directly from logsBoards, dimension lumber, timbers
Manufactured woodWood fibers altered/transformed and glued togetherGlulam, LVL, PSL, OSB, plywood, MDF
Disadvantages of wood

✘ Can't take stresses as high as steel or reinforced concrete
✘ Natural defects reduce strength
✘ Reacts to moisture — swells, shrinks, warps, splits, twists
✘ Burns readily
✘ Insects & decay in moist conditions without protection

6.2

Softwood vs. hardwood — it's about the TREE

On the exam

"Softwood" and "hardwood" refer to groups of trees — conifer vs. broadleaf — not how hard a board is. (Balsa is a hardwood!)

SoftwoodHardwood
StructureSimpler, coarser grainMore complex, finer/interesting grain & color
Growth2–3× faster → plentiful, cheaperSlower, less plentiful, costlier
UsesFraming (studs, joists, rafters), millwork, siding, decksFine furniture, cabinets, doors, trim, paneling, finished floors
SpeciesPine, spruce, fir, hemlock; cedar, cypress, redwoodOak, maple, walnut, birch, poplar, cherry, mahogany (floors: oak, maple, ash)
Why cedar outside?

Cedar's heartwood naturally resists decay — so it's chosen for siding, decks, fences. Cypress and redwood too. Other species (like pine) need pressure treatment to force in a preservative.

Hook

Cedar = decay-resistant "cedar chest" wood. Softwood = Stick with needles (conifer). Hardwood = Has broad leaves.

Quick check
Which statement about softwood and hardwood is correct?
Conifer vs. broadleaf — a botanical grouping.

Lumber production — the 7 steps

6.4

Sawing: plain vs. quarter

Plain sawing: a series of parallel cuts straight through the log, so boards are cut roughly tangent to the growth rings. Max yield and cheapest — but the varied grain makes boards distort as they dry. Used for framing lumber (it's hidden, strong enough, and economical).

Quarter (radial) sawing: log is quartered first, then cuts are made perpendicular to the rings → vertical grain. Less yield, but consistent grain, wears better, and distorts less. Example: vertical-grain fir decking.

Practice Q15

"Plain sawing cuts boards roughly tangent to the growth rings, often in a series of parallel cuts through the log."

6.5

Seasoning (drying): air vs. kiln

Seasoning = drying lumber. Why? Unseasoned wood is heavier, weaker, and less dimensionally stable.

Seasoning brings moisture content to 19% or less. Lumber above 19% = "green lumber", which shrinks more after it's installed in a dry (indoor) environment — bad for finish carpentry.

Drying effects: pieces shrink · weight drops · strength & stiffness increase · more dimensionally stable. Shrinkage along the length is negligible; across the grain it's measurable (most in the tangential direction).

On the exam

Kiln drying uses a chamber to control temperature, humidity, and airflow — fast (days). Air drying uses ambient conditions and generally takes longer (months).

Quick check
Which drying method uses a chamber to control temperature, humidity, and airflow?
Kiln = controlled chamber.
6.6

Surfacing & grading

Surfacing (planing) gives smooth surfaces and dimensional precision. Softwoods: S4S (all 4 sides). Hardwoods: often S2S. Done after drying — because lumber shrinks while it dries, so you plane it to final size afterward.

Grading evaluates each piece for strength & stiffness and straightness, by people or machines (cameras + computers). Grade depends on species, number and kinds of defects, and grain direction. Higher grade = higher allowable stress = higher price. (No. 2 & 3 are most common for framing.)

Grade stamp shows

Mill code · grading agency · species · seasoning method · moisture condition when surfaced · structural grade. e.g. "KD-19" = kiln dried to 19% or less; "SYP" = Southern Yellow Pine.

6.7

Nominal vs. actual dimensions

Lumber is sold by nominal size ("in name only"). It's rough-sawn close to that size, then dried (shrinks) and surfaced (planed) — so the actual piece is smaller.

Nominal sizeActual is…Example
1"¼" less1 → ¾
2" – 6"½" less4 → 3½, 6 → 5½
over 6"¾" less8 → 7¼, 12 → 11¼
Practice Q17 answers — memorize

1 × 6 board = ¾ × 5½"
2 × 8 dimension lumber = 1½ × 7¼"
8 × 8 timber = 7½ × 7½"

Heads-up on timbers

The practice key uses standard timber dressing: ½" less each way (8×8 → 7½×7½). The calculator follows that for anything 5"+ thick. The lecture's "over 6 → ¾ less" rule is for boards & dimension lumber widths (lecture example: 6×8 → 5½ × 7¼). If a timber shows up, go with ½" less like the key.

Lengths are not reduced: a 10-ft board is 10 ft. Why the break at 6"? Wider pieces shrink and distort more, so more is planed off.

Quick check
Actual dimensions of a nominal 1×6 board and a 2×8, both surfaced dry?
1 → ¾ ; 6 → 5½ ; 2 → 1½ ; 8 → 7¼.
6.8

Size classes: boards · dimension lumber · timbers

Classified by nominal thickness.

  • Widths: dimension lumber 4, 6, 8, 10, 12"; boards 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12"
  • Lengths: 8', 10', 12', 14', 16' — 2-ft increments (nominal = actual for length)
  • Special: 93" precut studs; special-order lengths (2×10s commonly to 20 ft)
